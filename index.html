About 2 minutes. Template: what is the dissertation research question; explain it and why it is important (3 slides).
Dissertation paper: “Still Buying Local? Investor Offices, First Financings, and Venture Exits in U.S. Regions, 2005–2025.”
Chen, Gompers, Kovner and Lerner (2010, Journal of Urban Economics) documented the concentration of U.S. venture capital in San Francisco, Boston and New York over 1975–2005. Two relationships underpin their account: areas with higher prior venture success host more offices, and areas with more offices record more newly financed startups. Together: the catch-22. A region needs a record of success to attract investors but lacks the local investors to build that record.
Their proposed way in: center-based investors had higher IPO rates, especially on deals outside their home regions, so peripheral regions might benefit more from reducing frictions for remote investors than from cultivating local firms (Chen et al. 2009, p. 15; 2010, p. 101).
Why it matters: concentration persists. The three centers hold 52.6% of observed offices in 2024 (55.0% in 2005). [Your words: why this question matters to you, for example Atlanta.]

About 2 minutes. Why revisit 2005–2025:
• Exit routes changed. Public listings declined relative to trade sales (Gao et al. 2013; Doidge et al. 2017); late-stage private capital let companies stay private longer (Ewens and Farre-Mensa 2020). In our sample, of companies first financed 2005–2009, 7.3% listed and 51.7% were acquired without listing; listings are 9–12% of exits in every cohort. Chen measured success by IPOs.
• Measurement changed. Exempt reporting venture advisers enter Form ADV from 2012, so office presence can be dated in filings.
• Questions the original equations leave open. Contemporaneous office and startup counts mix differences between regions with changes within them; success based on eventual outcomes of earlier cohorts differs from exits already realized when an office opens. We keep Chen's specifications for comparison and add dated exits and regional panels.

About 2 minutes.
RQ1. Regional success and investor presence: how is regional success associated with offices and branch openings? Re-estimate Chen's area office-count regressions and firm–region branch-opening models; distinguish aggregate regional success from an investor's own regional record; ask whether dated listings precede later office counts.
RQ2. Local offices and first financings: how do offices, first financings and exits covary between and within areas? Pooled vs area fixed effects; three-equation panel VAR.
RQ3. Investor location and nonlocal capital: how do the center premium and subsequent regional activity vary with outside investment? Listings and acquisitions are estimated separately. This tests the evidence behind Chen's proposed nonlocal-investor alternative.
These are questions about observed relationships and their timing; the paper makes no causal claim.

About 2 minutes. Template: highlight literature and theory.
1. VC geography, distance and interregional capital flows: Chen et al. (2010) set the benchmark regularities; coinvestment networks extend spatial reach (Sorenson and Stuart 2001; Hochberg et al. 2007, 2010); proximity matters after investment (Bernstein et al. 2016, airline routes); local capital supply shapes startup formation and relocation (Samila and Sorenson 2011; Chen and Ewens 2025).
2. Organizational histories, exit modes and retained capacity: founders spawned by established firms (Gompers et al. 2005); IPOs and acquisitions differ in local spillovers (Stuart and Sorenson 2003); listings are 9–12 percent of recorded outcomes in every 2005–2025 cohort (paper Table 1).
3. Entrepreneurial ecosystems and temporal precedence: outputs feed back into local resources (Stam 2015; Spigel 2017; Spigel and Harrison 2018); comparative work must separate scale between regions from precedence within them (Alvedalen and Boschma 2017).
Lens: between-area scale, within-area precedence (Granger 1969), deal-level outcomes by investor location.

Data: a unified multi-source dataset: PrivCo's October 2026 venture delivery; Crunchbase 2013 and 2015 exports; S&P Capital IQ private placements; SEC Form D and Form ADV; Ritter's venture-backed IPO series; Compustat/CRSP. Entities linked by CIK, EIN, normalized web domains and reviewed name-and-region rules. Sample: 130,054 first firm–company investments, 43,467 companies, 4,480 U.S. venture firms; 191 areas in the dynamic panel (3,820 area-years). Geography: 2009 OMB combined statistical areas.
Methods: (1) Chen's tables re-estimated, adding statistical-area fixed effects; (2) a three-equation panel VAR (offices N, new companies K, listings E) with area and year effects to read temporal precedence; (3) deal-level probits and linear probability models, including a common five-year follow-up sample (86,642 investments) so exit timing is equal across groups.

About 2½ minutes. Template: highlight the contribution.
RQ1: regional success remains positively associated with office counts across 173 areas (7.018, t = 2.26). The comparison mostly reflects hubs against other markets: 2.161 (s.e. 1.483) outside the twelve hubs, 2.280 with log 2010 population, and 0.138 with area fixed effects. Branch openings: own-firm and regional success cannot be distinguished under either dating, 0.138 [−0.283, 0.558] with pre-opening listings.
RQ2: area fixed effects cut the office–financing slope from 0.836 to 0.208 (−75%, comparable to Chen's −82%); the within-area association stays positive nationally and outside the hubs. Lagged offices precede first financings (0.172 activity-extended; 0.068 when dated by Form ADV filings alone, imprecise post-2012). Reverse precedence depends on the office measure: 0.019 vs 0.002 on identical 2013–2025 observations.
RQ3: center-based investors keep a 1.83-point listing advantage; their extra premium on outside deals is 0.28 [−0.77, 1.34], below Chen's 2.9–3.8. With five years of follow-up, completed IPOs are 1.19 (LPM) vs 0.11 (probit) and absent in proportion to baseline (Poisson ratio of ratios 0.80 [0.55, 1.15]). For acquisitions, the center premium is 2.54 points smaller on outside deals in both LPM and probit—driven by non-center investors' outside deals (+2.35 vs −0.19 for center investors) and measuring sale frequency rather than return (983 of 6,894 report deal value). Local entry-round co-investor +2.18 points.
Contribution: separates three things a pooled cross-section conflates: geographic association, temporal relationship within regions, and the investor-location premium under different exit definitions. Every estimate is an association.

About 1½ minutes. Template: how will this dissertation open new frontiers for you? Connect to your professional growth.
Atlanta (the Atlanta–Sandy Springs–Gainesville CSA), from the same national data: 1,769 first firm–company investments 2005–2025; 21.8% from firms with their main office in Atlanta, against 40.7% nationally (12% in 2025); active main offices grew from 19 (2005) to a peak of 62 (2022), 56 in 2025; 710 companies first financed; 20 dated listings, 218 dated exits.
Atlanta is a market where most capital is nonlocal, the setting Chen's catch-22 describes. The findings point to the next question: a local co-investor in the entry round goes with a 2.18-point higher listing probability, while the association of nonlocal center share with next-year local first financings is imprecisely estimated. Who co-invests with local capital, and how syndicates form, is the frontier.
